## Adaptive Functional Materials

The Gamage Lab seeks to uncover the fundamental principles that govern **adaptive behavior in functional materials** and use this understanding to create materials and devices with dynamically tunable properties.

We use **oxide** and **organic–inorganic hybrid materials** as complementary platforms to investigate how electronic, ionic, structural, optical, magnetic, and interfacial processes interact to produce emergent functionality.

Our research integrates **thin-film synthesis, nanofabrication, device measurements, and nanoscale characterization** to connect local material transformations with macroscopic functional behavior.

Rather than focusing on a single material system or application, we seek general design principles for materials whose properties can be deliberately modified by electrical, chemical, thermal, optical, or environmental stimuli.

---

## Adaptive Oxide Materials

We investigate correlated and ionically active oxide thin films as model systems for understanding adaptive electronic behavior.

Complex oxides provide a particularly rich materials platform because **ionic motion, electronic correlations, structural transformations, magnetism, defects, and interfaces** can be strongly coupled. Small changes in composition or local structure can therefore produce large changes in electronic functionality.

Our current work focuses on rare-earth nickelates and related transition-metal oxides, with particular interest in understanding the physical mechanisms governing resistive switching and memory behavior.

Key questions include:

- How does ionic motion modify local electronic structure and conductivity?
- Where do resistive-switching processes initiate, and how do they evolve spatially?
- How do defects and interfaces influence switching pathways?
- How do electrode and contact materials modify local transport and chemical processes?
- Can nanoscale material transformations be quantitatively connected with macroscopic electrical behavior?
- What materials-level design principles are required for reproducible adaptive electronic devices?

Our oxide research combines **thin-film deposition, micro/nanofabrication, electrical transport measurements, structural characterization, and nanoscale spectroscopy**.

## Adaptive Hybrid Materials

We develop **molecularly engineered organic–inorganic hybrid thin films** as a complementary platform for adaptive functionality.

Atomic layer deposition (ALD) and molecular layer deposition (MLD) enable molecular-scale control over film thickness, composition, bonding, and interfaces. By combining inorganic building blocks with organic molecular components, we aim to create materials whose properties can be engineered through both **chemical composition and molecular architecture**.

A central objective of this program is to understand how molecular-level structure governs macroscopic electrical, ionic, optical, mechanical, and environmental response.

Key questions include:

- How does molecular architecture influence electronic and ionic transport?
- Can organic components regulate ion motion, defect formation, or interfacial charge transfer?
- How do hybrid bonding networks influence hysteresis, memory, and stimulus response?
- Can hybrid materials exhibit adaptive behavior that is difficult to achieve in conventional inorganic systems?
- How can molecular-scale control of composition and interfaces be translated into reproducible device functionality?

Our hybrid-materials research integrates **ALD/MLD, complementary wet-chemical synthesis, thin-film characterization, device fabrication, and nanoscale spectroscopy**.

## Mechanistic Nanoscopy

Adaptive materials are often spatially heterogeneous. Phase transformations, defect migration, chemical reactions, and interfacial processes can occur over nanometer length scales and may remain hidden in conventional device-level measurements.

A central component of our research is therefore **mechanistic nanoscopy**: using nanoscale imaging and spectroscopy to identify where adaptive behavior originates, how it evolves, and how local transformations control macroscopic functionality.

Our approach includes:

- scanning probe microscopy;
- near-field optical microscopy;
- nano-infrared spectroscopy;
- spatially resolved electrical characterization;
- structural and spectroscopic analysis; and
- correlative measurements linking local materials behavior with device response.

Mechanistic nanoscopy provides the bridge between our oxide and hybrid materials programs by allowing us to interrogate the fundamental processes responsible for adaptive functionality across multiple material platforms.

## Integrated Research Approach

Our research follows an iterative experimental cycle:

**Synthesize → Fabricate → Measure → Resolve Mechanisms → Redesign**

We combine:

- thin-film synthesis by sputtering, ALD, and MLD;
- micro- and nanofabrication of electronic devices;
- electrical and physical characterization of materials and devices;
- nanoscale imaging and spectroscopy;
- mechanistic studies connecting local transformations to macroscopic behavior; and
- complementary modeling and theory through collaboration.

This integrated approach allows us to move beyond empirical materials optimization toward a **mechanistic and predictive understanding of adaptive functional materials**.

## Research Philosophy

We are interested not simply in whether a material exhibits an unusual response, but in **why that response emerges, how it evolves, and how it can be controlled**.

Our research philosophy is captured by three interconnected ideas:

**Discover.** Identify new materials, phenomena, and forms of adaptive behavior.

**Understand.** Determine the physical and chemical mechanisms that govern them.

**Adapt.** Use that understanding to engineer materials, interfaces, and devices with controlled functionality.
